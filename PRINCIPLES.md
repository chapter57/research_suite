# Research Suite: Guiding Principles

These principles guide every design decision in Research Suite (working name). They are adapted, in our own
words, from Jordan B. Peterson's *Essay Writing Guide*, chosen by the project's founder for its account of how
writing and thinking are connected. The guide's political views are not part of this project.

When a feature, a coach behavior or a piece of wording is in doubt, choose the option that does more to help
the student **think**.

## 1. Writing is thinking

The main reason to write is not to show a teacher what you know. It is to work out an informed, coherent set
of ideas about something that matters. Writing extends memory, lets you move and test ideas, and lets you
throw out weak ones until good ones remain.

**For the app:** the product exists to strengthen the student's thinking, not to produce text. The AI coach
never writes the paper. It asks, reacts, explains and points; the student decides and writes.

## 2. An essay is an attempt to answer a question

"Essay" means *attempt*. A good essay starts from a real question the writer wants answered, and choosing
that question is among the hardest and most important steps, even when the topic is assigned. Interest is a
signal: if writers are bored, they have likely chosen the wrong question or are approaching it the wrong way.

**For the app:** help students frame topics as questions they care about. Boredom and being stuck are worth
reflecting on, not hiding.

## 3. Read for understanding; take notes in your own words

Notes are a dialogue with the author: "this is what I understand you to be saying." Never copy a source word
for word into notes. Write the gist, ideally with the source closed, and record questions and disagreements.
Gather more material than you will use (roughly two to three times the final length), because writing well
requires knowing more than you say. If you can't write, you may have nothing to say yet: read more.

**For the app:** reading and note-taking tools should prompt paraphrase and response, not highlighting or
copying. Pasted text is a reason for reflection, not suspicion.

## 4. The outline is the argument

The outline is the essay's skeleton: about ten to fifteen sentences, roughly one per 100 words, with
sub-outlines for longer work. Getting the parts in the right order is one of the most important steps.

**For the app:** treat outlining as a first-class activity, not an optional extra.

## 5. Producing and editing are different jobs

Drafting produces; editing reduces and arranges. Doing both at once stalls both. Draft quickly and roughly,
aim about 25% longer than the final length so there is something to cut, and skip ahead when stuck.

**For the app:** support a drafting mindset (no nitpicking, momentum first) separately from an editing
mindset. The coach should not polish grammar while a student is still finding their ideas.

## 6. Writing works at many levels at once

Word, sentence, paragraph, order of paragraphs, the essay as a whole, and beyond that the reader and the
reader's culture. An essay can be correct at every small level and still fail because it isn't interesting
or important, and a clumsy essay can succeed because it holds a real idea. Writing is for oneself *and* for an
audience.

**For the app:** coaching, reflection prompts and feedback should be clear about which level they address,
and should keep the whole essay and the reader in view, not only surface errors.

## 7. Revise sentence by sentence, then paragraph by paragraph

Put each sentence on its own line, write a better version beneath it, and keep whichever is better. Better
usually means shorter (cut 15 to 25%), plainer and more exact: words you would comfortably say aloud. Read
sentences aloud. Then put sentences in the best order within each paragraph, cut what is unnecessary, and
then reorder the paragraphs themselves.

**For the app:** revision tools should make comparing versions and reordering easy, and keep the earlier
version visible so the student can judge whether the change truly improved things.

## 8. Re-outline from memory

After a full draft, write a new outline without looking at the essay. Memory filters out what is
unimportant and keeps what is vital. Rebuild the essay on the new outline and discard what no longer fits.
Repeat with fresh eyes, ideally days later. The work is finished when a rewrite is no longer clearly better.

**For the app:** offer exercises like re-outlining with the draft hidden. The process recording should help
students see how their argument changed between drafts.

## 9. Conventions first, then mastery

Conventions (paragraph length, citation style, formatting) make writing easier for readers. Learn them well
before breaking them; break them only deliberately, for a reason. Cite every fact and borrowed idea.

**For the app:** teach conventions as tools that serve readers. Course packs set the specific conventions
(ENGL 102 uses MLA and treats style and dialect as rhetorical choices), and these principles set the spirit.

## 10. Show up, and finish

Write a little every day rather than waiting for big blocks of free time. The mind resists at first and
settles after about fifteen minutes of refusing distraction. Two or three focused hours are a practical
maximum. Finished beats perfect: a handed-in essay is worth far more than an unwritten one.

**For the app:** encourage short, regular sessions and make it easy to return. Process stats should
celebrate steady work, and nothing in the app should make a student feel the draft must be perfect before
it can be shared.

## Tone

Student-facing text should carry these ideas in a warm, encouraging voice. Keep the conviction that writing
is serious and worthwhile, but not the source's harsher phrasing.
